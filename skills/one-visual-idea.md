# Choosing the one visual idea

Every good tool here has one picture at its centre that matches how people already imagine the
thing: seats look like seats, a bill looks like a receipt, a road trip looks like a ticket, who
owes who looks like arrows between faces. Everything else on the card (header, a line of status,
one button) serves that picture. Two pictures compete; none makes the tool feel generic.

## Find the object in the world

Ask: if these friends sorted this out at a table with paper, what would be on the table?

| The moment | The object | Not this |
|---|---|---|
| Seats at a show, tables at formal | a seat map or round tables with faces in the seats | a list of names with dropdowns |
| A bill with items | a receipt, each line with the faces who share it | a form with amount fields |
| Many IOUs after a trip | faces in a ring with arrows, amounts on the arrows | a table of debts |
| Rides somewhere at set times | cars with seat slots on a line of leave times | a sign-up list |
| A road trip or flight | a boarding pass with a tear-off stub | a card with three text lines |
| Picking among places | place cards with a small drawn thumbnail, price and the chat's note | bare option rows |
| When can everyone go | a week strip or heat grid of who is free | a poll per day |
| A bracket | the bracket tree, winners moving right | a sequence of polls |
| A streak or challenge | a row of day cells per person, filled as they check in | a counter |
| A game | the board itself, full width | a description of the game |
| A semester recap | tap-through cards, one big number or fact each | a bulleted list |

## When rows are right

Rows (OptionRow, a claim list) are the honest picture when the items have no shape of their own
and the job is "pick one" or "take one": four dinner spots with nothing but names, shifts by hour,
a packing list. Rows also win when there are more than about 12 items. Then make the rows good:
the viewer's row tinted, faces on each row, counts not percentages, and one thing that is special
to this moment (a thumbnail per place, the hour as a time block, the owner's chat quote as a
detail line).

Do not force a picture where the moment has none. A drawn map for "who's bringing ice" is noise.

## Size the picture to the stakes

- Light and playful (dishes, costumes, pictionary): the picture can be bold and take the card.
- Practical and time-bound (a ride at dawn): the picture is plain and the facts are big.
- Money: the numbers are the picture; the drawing frames them (a receipt edge, arrows), it never
  hides them.

## Worked example: who's in for the ski trip

Chat: Breckenridge, two nights, leaving Thursday 5 pm from the dorm lot, $180 each for the
condo, Bo says he can drive six.

The object is a lift ticket: a tall pass on a lanyard with the mountain's name, the dates and the
price, and a row of punch holes, one per person who is in. Tapping "I'm in" punches your hole with
your face in it; when the condo is full (six), the pass shows "Full · 6 going" and the button
becomes "Waitlist". One picture, one action, one satisfying moment (the punch).

## Worked example: book club pages

The object is a bookshelf: one short shelf per person, spines filling left to right as they log
pages (each spine is 50 pages), the book's title on the shelf edge. Logging is one tap of "+25" or
"+50". A table of numbers does the job; the shelf reads instantly and makes progress feel real.

## Checks before you move on

- Can you say the idea in five words ("receipt with faces per line")?
- Is it something the group would recognise without a caption?
- Does the one action happen on the picture itself (tap a seat, tap a line, tap an arrow)? The
  best tools put the interaction in the picture, not in a button under it.
